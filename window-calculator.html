Equipment, house, and pricing
Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio: cooling delivered per unit of electricity, in Btu per watt-hour. Higher is better. Printed on the yellow EnergyGuide sticker on the outdoor unit; if the sticker is gone, search the model number on the outdoor unit's data plate. Systems from the 1990s are typically 10–12, 2006–2022 installs 13–16, and anything sold since 2023 is at least 14–15 (SEER2 ratings are ~5% lower than SEER; either is fine here).
Yellow EnergyGuide sticker or model-number lookup. Old units 10–12, recent 14–16. More
Integrated Energy Factor: liters of water removed per kilowatt-hour of electricity. Higher is better. Listed on the ENERGY STAR label, the spec sheet, or the retailer's product page. If you only know pints/day and watts: IEF ≈ pints × 0.473 ÷ (watts × 24 ÷ 1000). Federal minimum is 1.30 (≤25 pints) to 1.60 (25–50 pints); ENERGY STAR units are about 1.6–2.0.

How many times per hour the outdoor air replaces the room's air. Hard to know precisely; use a bracket. Windows cracked, still air: about 0.5–1. Several windows open, light breeze: 1–3. Cross-breeze through opposite windows: 3–6. Window fan or whole-house fan: fan CFM × 60 ÷ volume in ft³ (a 20-inch box fan is roughly 1,000–2,000 CFM). Energy for this session

Method: saturation vapor pressure from the Buck (1981) equation; humidity ratio W = 0.62198·Pw/(P−Pw); moist-air enthalpy h = 1.006·T + W·(2501 + 1.86·T) kJ/kg; latent heat ≈ 2465 kJ/kg. In AC mode, the total load through the coil is m·Δh and electricity is load ÷ COP. In dehumidifier mode, imported moisture costs (liters ÷ IEF) kWh, and all of that electricity plus the latent heat of the condensed water is released into the room as heat. Everything here is a steady-state estimate that ignores thermal mass, fan energy, and infiltration you'd have anyway.
